# TTA Module 1 · Chapters 1–5 (Inside a Market → Interest & Bond Pricing)
Condensed notes in my own words. `[Claude+]` = my additions.

## Ch 1 · Inside a Market
**1.1 What a market is**: any venue where buyers and sellers trade; price = the latest level where both agreed, not set from above. Two jobs: channel savings to users of capital, and **price discovery**. Continuous auction: excess buyers lift the offer until sellers appear. Example type: a surprise supply cut makes buyers pay up step by step until balance returns. Core theme: **prices move when opinions change, and opinions change on new information; expected news doesn't move markets.** Takeaway: you trade against other people's opinions; your job is understanding why their verdict could change.
**1.2 Who is in the market**: central banks (policy, not profit), pensions/insurers (long-term returns), hedge funds (leveraged, often short-term), market makers (earn the spread), corporates (hedging for certainty), retail (small share). Much flow is *non-speculative* (hedging, rebalancing, mandated). Investors vs traders by horizon. Case: GameStop Jan 2021, where a crowded short was forced to buy back and fuelled the rally. Takeaway: position around what big players *must* do.
**1.3 How a trade happens**: bid (best buyer), ask (best seller), spread = cost paid up front; buy at the ask, sell at the bid. Order book: market orders consume resting size level by level → **buying mechanically lifts price**; big orders or thin books = big moves. Exchange (central book, e.g. index futures) vs OTC (dealer quotes: most FX and bonds). Pip = 0.0001 on most pairs. Spot gold is dealer-quoted with a tight spread in normal conditions.
**1.4 Liquidity, volume, volatility**: liquidity = trade size without moving price (tight spread, deep book); volume = how much trades; volatility = size of swings. Volatility often *comes from* vanishing liquidity. Around big news: market makers pull quotes → thin book → jumps. Case: March 2020 dash for cash, when even Treasuries became hard to trade until the Fed bought. Liquidity disappears exactly when needed. Size for the "pool" you're in.

## Ch 2 · Positions and Price Moves
**2.1 Long, short, leverage**: long = profit if it rises; short = profit if it falls. In FX every trade is long one currency and short the other. Long loss is floored at zero; short loss is open-ended → **short squeezes**. Standard lot EURUSD ≈ $10/pip; 100 pips ≈ $1,000. Leverage = borrowed exposure (10:1 turns a 1% move into 10% of equity) → margin calls well before wipeout. Forced deleveraging drives many sharp moves. Case: Archegos 2021, whose hidden leverage via swaps led to forced bank sales (Credit Suisse ~$5bn loss). *Leverage removes your option to wait.* Takeaway: set risk in money ("what do I lose on a 2% move?").
**2.2 The trading day**: Asia (quieter; JPY, AUD) → London (largest FX hub, open surge) → New York (US data; afternoons thin). **London–NY overlap ≈ 13:00–16:00 UK = most liquid.** Major US data at 13:30 UK: spreads widen, then jumps. Thin-hour moves often fade.
**2.3 Why prices actually move**: price = the consensus belief about the future, backed by money. Moves come from *changes in belief*. Same action, opposite results: a hike that matches expectations can see the currency drift lower (profit-taking); a hike *smaller* than expected makes it fall hard. Case: US CPI 10 Nov 2022, a slightly softer print than forecast → S&P +5%+ in a day, USD down. *Less bad than expected can be bullish.* The three questions (expected? wrong? what reprices?) are introduced here. Honest caveat: expectations aren't always visible, and markets can stay wrong for a long time.

## Ch 3 · Mapping the Markets
**3.1 The map**: five arenas: equities, bonds (the "quiet giant", larger than equities), currencies (~$7tn/day turnover, the largest), commodities (mostly futures), derivatives (huge notional ≠ money at risk). Government borrowing sets the base cost of money, which feeds mortgages, loans and valuations. The arenas are wired together, and moves often *start in bonds*.
**3.2 Equities & indices**: an index = cap-weighted basket, so you trade aggregate earnings and mood. Composition matters: Nasdaq 100 (tech), DAX (autos, industrials, chemicals), FTSE 100 (banks, oil, miners; mostly overseas revenue), Nikkei (exporters, moves with the yen). Three macro drivers: **expected profits, the discount rate (bond yields), risk appetite**. Higher yields → future profits discounted harder → index falls even with earnings unchanged; tech ("long duration") hit hardest. Case: 2022, with profits intact but S&P −~20% and Nasdaq 100 −~33% on rates.
**3.3 Bonds**: a tradeable loan with coupon, maturity and face value. The safest borrower's yield (US Treasuries) = the reference price of money. Government deficit → bond sales → yield demanded → benchmark for all borrowing. Fixed coupon but moving price → **price up = yield down** (set-up for ch 5).

## Ch 4 · Currencies, Commodities and Contracts
**4.1 FX**: pairs quote base in terms of quote (EURUSD 1.0850 = one euro costs $1.0850). Largest market; every cross-border transaction passes through it. Users: trade, investors, tourists, CBs, speculators, and **investment and speculative flows dwarf trade flows**. Tug-of-war image: the pair shows who's winning, not which side changed. Inflows strengthen a currency, outflows weaken it; steered mostly by **rate and growth differentials**.
**4.2 Commodities**: supply/demand with storage in between. Copper = growth gauge; oil = inflation input (slow supply response → big swings; oil → CPI → rate expectations → all assets). **Gold = no yield, a store of value; its rival is the yield available elsewhere, so falling yields and fear support it.** The same boom headline can lift copper and leave gold flat. Case: Europe 2022 gas shock (gas ~€300/MWh peak vs ~€20 before) → trade surplus flipped to deficit → EURUSD below parity.
**4.3 Futures, options, ETFs**: futures are standardised, exchange-traded and margined (leverage built in) with **expiry** (cash or physical settlement; roll to stay in). Contract maths: WTI 1,000 bbl ($1 = $1,000/contract), gold 100oz ($1 = $100), E-mini S&P $50/point. Beginner trap: one contract on a small account turns a normal move into a huge % loss. Case: WTI negative (~−$37) on 20 Apr 2020 when storage was full ahead of expiry. Options = right, not obligation; the buyer's max loss is the premium. ETFs = baskets that trade like shares; their flows are a signal. Know three numbers before trading futures: size, $ per point, expiry.

## Ch 5 · Interest and Bond Pricing
**5.1 Interest**: the price of time + default risk (longer and riskier = higher). Central banks set the shortest rate; markets set the rest via bonds. Safe government rate = **risk-free rate**, the ruler everything else is measured against.
**5.2 Government bonds**: face value, coupon, maturity; trade continuously. Treasuries (US), Bunds (DE), Gilts (UK), JGBs (JP). Euro area: shared currency, separate issuers. **Italy–Germany (BTP–Bund) spread** = a eurozone stress gauge.
**5.3 Price vs yield**: yield ≈ coupon ÷ price. Fixed coupon + a lower price = higher yield (e.g. $40 on $950 ≈ 4.2%; on $1,050 ≈ 3.8%). When new bonds pay more, old bonds must fall in price to compete. Case: 2022, the worst Treasury year on record (10y ~1.5% → >4%; long-bond funds −~⅓). *"Safe" = repaid, not price-stable.*

## Practice questions (my own, in the course's style)
1. You buy 2 standard lots of GBPUSD at 1.2700 and close at 1.2655. Result? → −45 pips × $20 ≈ −$900
2. A central bank cuts 25bp when 50bp was widely expected. Most likely currency reaction? → Rises (a hawkish surprise vs expectations)
3. Gold futures: long 1 contract at 2,400, exit at 2,385. P&L? → −$1,500
4. A bond with a $50 coupon trades at $1,100. Approximate current yield? → ~4.5%
5. EURUSD spread widens from 1 to 6 pips seconds before NFP. Best explanation? → Market makers pulling quotes; the book is thinning before scheduled volatility
6. The Italy–Bund spread widens 60bp in a week. What is the market signalling? → Rising stress about a weaker eurozone member (and the euro)
