# 00.1 · Reading a Country (Layer 1 deep dive)
`[Claude+]` = my additions.

## Why start here
A country's institutions (government, central bank, legal system) are the **pipes** that transmit capital, volatility and risk. When a shock hits, they decide where the pressure goes. You want a *simple, sourced* profile, not a dossier. Free sources: IMF country pages, CIA World Factbook, World Bank data.
The skill isn't knowing facts; it's **connecting them into probable outcomes**. The seven ingredients overlap: a country is one system.

## 1. Demographics
- Population pyramid: a wide base (young, growing, e.g. US) vs a top-heavy shape (ageing, e.g. Italy, Japan).
- **Channel 1, labour/inflation**: fewer working-age people → scarcer labour → wages ↑ → inflation. An ageing country's central bank has a very different job.
- **Channel 2, spending mix**: 30-year-olds buy homes, furniture, childcare; 70-year-olds buy healthcare and hold savings. Compare age structure with the spending mix to find pressure points (e.g. many first-time buyers vs limited housing).
> `[Claude+]` Ageing also tends to raise savings and lower the neutral rate (Japan, Europe). That's one reason those currencies became funding currencies for carry trades.

## 2. Output capacity
- What the economy *can* produce (factories, energy, infrastructure, skills).
- **Capacity rule**: a demand shock with little spare capacity → inflation; with lots of spare capacity → mostly growth.
- Years of underinvestment quietly shrink capacity and show up later as inflation. Ask: "If demand jumped tomorrow, could supply keep up?"
> `[Claude+]` Proxies: capacity utilisation (US ~ 78–80% is normal; above ~82% is tight), output-gap estimates (IMF/OECD/CBO), and the capex share of GDP.

## 3. Geography ("geography is destiny")
- Coasts/ports (cheap trade; landlocked = permanent cost), navigable rivers (near-free transport), neighbours (trade vs defence spending and risk premium), chokepoints (straits and canals that reprice shipping and risk when threatened).
- US example: two ocean moats, the Mississippi river system, vast farmland.
- It's easy to forget because it never changes, then it dominates during wars and blockades.

## 4. Resources, weather, transport, tech
- **Long/short each commodity** (read from trade data): long = net exporter, short = net importer.
- Classic pair: Canada long oil, Japan short oil → **CADJPY tends to track crude**. Build this map for every major commodity.
- Weather → seasonality (natural gas in winter, harvests, tourism).
- Transport → use freight/shipping data, not headlines; data shows change before narratives do.
- Tech → a productivity multiplier; slow but compounding.
> `[Claude+]` Other long/short pairs worth knowing: AUD (iron ore, coal, LNG) vs CNY demand; NOK long oil vs SEK not; CLP/PEN long copper; ZAR long gold/PGMs; INR/TRY short oil (oil spikes hurt them). For gold: AUD has some gold-export sensitivity, but the dominant gold driver is USD real yields, not trade flows.

## 5. Politics & geopolitics
- The key question is **incentives**: are those in power rewarded for growing the whole pie, or protecting a narrow group? That shapes long-run prosperity.
- Practical checks: populism and inequality, biases in spending, what the dominant parties want.
- Geopolitics: *given all the constraints, which outcomes are actually likely?*

## 6. The three regimes
For each: **how are decisions made, and by whom?**
- **Currency**: float vs peg/managed. It decides what adjusts under pressure (pegs suppress moves until they break).
- **Monetary**: is the central bank independent or politically steered? Captured central banks tolerate more inflation.
- **Fiscal**: who controls taxing/spending, and with what bias → inflation and debt paths.
- Example: the 2023 BoJ governor change repriced global markets because Japan's reaction function suddenly became an open question.

## Country scorecard (7 questions)
1. History: how did it get here?
2. Demographics: gaining or losing workers/spenders?
3. Capacity: can supply meet a demand jump?
4. Geography: does the map help or hurt?
5. Resources: long/short which commodities?
6. Politics: who holds power, with which incentives?
7. Regimes: how are FX, rate and budget decisions made?

## Takeaways
Institutions = transmission pipes · facts → probable outcomes · keep a source list per ingredient · use primary data · always map to a price · it's one system.

## Self-test
1. Why would an ageing population be inflationary through one channel and disinflationary through another? → Labour scarcity pushes wages up; higher saving and lower demand push the other way
2. Two countries get the same demand shock. One has 85% capacity utilisation, the other 72%. Which gets more inflation? → The 85% one
3. Oil +20%. Which way should CADJPY go and why? → Up: CAD earns oil revenue, JPY pays for oil imports
4. What are the three regimes, and the one question to ask of each?
5. Why did a BoJ leadership change move global bond markets? → Uncertainty about Japan's policy reaction function; JGB yields and yen carry are global anchors
6. Name three free primary sources for a country profile.
